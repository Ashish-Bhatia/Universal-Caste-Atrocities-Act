SC/ST Act, BSA crosswalk

Evidence, burden and statutory-presumption interfaces.

SC/ST Act and Rules BSA Evidence / Presumption Crosswalk

Date: 2026-09-05

1. Scope and method

This artifact maps the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 and materially relevant Rules evidence/document interfaces against the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (BSA).

Scope:

  • Act s.8 presumptions.
  • Evidence-related aspects of Act s.3(2), principally false/fabricated evidence under s.3(2)(i)-(ii), disappearance of evidence/false information under s.3(2)(vi), and the proof interfaces of the remaining aggravated offences where evidence-law rules are materially relevant.
  • Rules interfaces involving information/records, spot-inspection and relief reports, investigation records, victim/witness participation, medical/post-mortem material and other documentary/electronic interfaces identified in the consolidated Rules extraction.
  • BSA definitions, relevancy, oral evidence, documentary evidence, public documents, presumptions as to documents, burden of proof, witnesses, examination/production and electronic records.
  • BSA repeal/savings and the transition from the Indian Evidence Act, 1872.
  • Ministry of Law and Justice S.O. 2790(E), dated 16-07-2024, as a controlled transition instrument for legacy Indian Evidence Act references.

Method: identify the special-law evidence or presumption rule, identify the closest BSA provision or provisions, distinguish direct correspondence from general evidentiary support, record residual differences and transition basis, and identify duplication, conflict, gap and ambiguity. No constitutional, policy or Bill-drafting conclusion is made.

2. Primary transition basis

The BSA, Act No. 47 of 2023, came into force on 01-07-2024. It applies to judicial proceedings in or before a Court, subject to the exclusions in s.1(2). BSA s.170(1) repeals the Indian Evidence Act, 1872. BSA s.170(2) preserves the repealed Act for applications, trials, inquiries, investigations, proceedings and appeals that were pending immediately before commencement. citehttps://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/20063/1/aa202347.pdf

S.O. 2790(E), dated 16-07-2024, issued by the Legislative Department under s.8 of the General Clauses Act, provides that references to the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 or provisions thereof in specified Acts, rules, regulations, orders and notifications in force are to be read as references to the BSA and corresponding provisions construed accordingly. It therefore supplies a transition rule for legacy Evidence Act references in the SC/ST Act and Rules. This does not itself establish substantive equivalence between every former Evidence Act provision and a BSA provision. citehttps://egazette.gov.in/WriteReadData/2024/255467.pdf

The SC/ST Act's current consolidated text still contains legacy criminal-law references. For the evidence workstream, the critical distinction is between (1) a special evidentiary presumption created by the SC/ST Act itself and (2) general rules of evidence supplied by the BSA. S.O. 2790(E) addresses references to the repealed Evidence Act; it does not repeal or replace a substantive presumption independently created by the SC/ST Act.

4. BSA definitions and general proof architecture

4.1 Definitions

BSA s.2 defines “document” broadly and expressly includes electronic and digital records. Its illustrations include emails, server logs, computer/smartphone records, messages, websites, locational evidence and voicemail stored on digital devices. “Evidence” includes statements, including electronically given statements, permitted or required before the Court, and documents including electronic/digital records produced for inspection. The definitions of “may presume” and “shall presume” establish the operative evidentiary consequences of those phrases when used by the Adhiniyam. BSA s.2(2) imports meanings from the Information Technology Act, BNSS and BNS for undefined expressions. citehttps://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/20063/1/aa202347.pdf

For Act s.8, the important point is that the BSA's definition of “shall presume” describes the consequence of a statutory direction to presume unless disproved. The Act's own words therefore remain controlling as the source of the special presumption, while the BSA supplies the general evidentiary environment in which that presumption operates.

4.2 Relevancy

BSA ss.3-14 establish the general relevance gateway. Material interfaces for SC/ST Act prosecutions include same-transaction facts, cause/effect, motive/preparation/conduct, state of mind, and facts bearing on whether conduct was accidental or intentional. These provisions are particularly relevant to proving intent/knowledge in s.3(2)(i), (ii) and (vi), but they do not alter the statutory elements of those offences. citehttps://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/20063/1/aa202347.pdf

BSA ss.29-31 also address entries in public records/electronic records, maps/charts/plans, and statements as to matters of public nature contained in Acts or notifications. These provisions may be materially relevant to official records and notifications used to establish public facts, statutory status or administrative actions, subject to the particular evidentiary requirements.

4.3 Oral and documentary evidence

BSA ss.54-60 govern proof of facts by oral evidence, direct oral evidence, proof of document contents, primary evidence, secondary evidence and circumstances permitting secondary evidence. The Act and Rules do not create a general exemption from these requirements. citehttps://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/20063/1/aa202347.pdf

BSA ss.74-77 distinguish public and private documents and provide rules for certified copies and proof of official documents. BSA ss.78-93 establish specified presumptions concerning certified copies, court records, Gazettes, electronic Gazettes, Government maps/plans, law books/reports, powers of attorney, electronic agreements, secure electronic records/signatures, electronic messages and older documents. These presumptions are document-specific and should not be treated as a general presumption of truth of an SC/ST Act complaint, police report or administrative report. citehttps://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/20063/1/aa202347.pdf

5. Electronic records

BSA s.61 states that an electronic or digital record is not inadmissible merely because it is electronic/digital and gives it the same legal effect, validity and enforceability as another document, subject to s.63. BSA s.62 directs proof of electronic-record contents through s.63. BSA s.63 provides the admissibility framework for computer outputs and specifies conditions concerning regular use, ordinary-course input, proper operation and reproduction/derivation of information. Section 63(4) requires a certificate to accompany electronic records at each instance of submission for admission, with the statutory particulars and the certificate form in the Schedule. citehttps://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/20063/1/aa202347.pdf

This is materially relevant to Act s.4(2)(f), which expressly refers to documents/electronic records, and to Act s.15A and Rules provisions involving video recording, communications and records. The SC/ST Act does not create a general electronic-evidence exception. BSA s.61-63 therefore supplies the general admissibility/proof framework unless another applicable law provides otherwise.

The BSA text also treats multiple stored electronic records, proper-custody records and simultaneous stored/transmitted video recordings as forms of primary evidence in specified circumstances. These rules should be applied from the current BSA text rather than by importing the former Evidence Act section numbers.

6. Burden of proof and Act s.8

BSA ss.104-107 establish the general allocation of burden. Section 104 places the burden on the person asserting facts on which the requested legal right/liability depends; s.105 places the burden on the person who would fail if no evidence were given; s.106 places the burden concerning a particular fact on the person who wishes the Court to believe it unless another law assigns it differently. Section 107 addresses facts required to make other evidence admissible. citehttps://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/20063/1/aa202347.pdf

BSA s.108 places on the accused the burden of proving circumstances bringing the case within BNS general exceptions or a special exception/proviso contained in the law defining the offence, with the Court presuming their absence. Section 109 concerns facts especially within a person's knowledge. These are general statutory burden rules. citehttps://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/20063/1/aa202347.pdf

Act s.8 is different. It is an express special-law presumption triggered by proof of the facts specified in clauses (a) or (b). The BSA does not contain a general equivalent for these SC/ST Act presumptions. The correct Phase 1 position is therefore:

  • Act s.8 remains the source of the special presumption.
  • BSA ss.104-109 govern general burden/proof questions around the case, subject to the special allocation created by Act s.8.
  • BSA s.2(l)'s definition of “shall presume” provides useful interpretive context but does not convert Act s.8 into a BSA provision.
  • The exact judicial operation, evidentiary threshold and standard for rebuttal of Act s.8 should be addressed in the Phase 2 case-law corpus, not inferred here.

7. BSA presumptions that are not equivalents of Act s.8

BSA Chapter V contains document-specific presumptions, and Chapter VII contains presumptions relating to certain offences and specified facts. BSA s.115 concerns certain offences in disturbed areas or areas with extensive public-peace disturbance and is not an equivalent of Act s.8. It should not be cited as the replacement or analogue of the SC/ST Act presumption. citehttps://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/20063/1/aa202347.pdf

BSA s.119 permits the Court to presume existence of certain facts having regard to the common course of natural events, human conduct and public/private business. This is a general permissive evidentiary power and is not a statutory replacement for Act s.8's mandatory special presumptions. The distinction between “may presume” and “shall presume” is material. citehttps://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/20063/1/aa202347.pdf

8. Witness interface

BSA s.124 provides that all persons are competent to testify unless the Court considers them unable to understand questions or give rational answers for the statutory reasons. BSA ss.125-139 address witnesses and related matters, including persons unable to communicate verbally, privilege, official communications, professional communications, accomplices and number of witnesses. BSA ss.140-160 govern examination, cross-examination, previous statements and related witness treatment. citehttps://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/20063/1/aa202347.pdf

The SC/ST Act and Rules contain victim/witness protection and participation rights, but they do not replace the BSA's general competency and examination rules. Act s.15A rights therefore operate as a special participation/protection layer, while the BSA supplies the evidentiary rules governing testimony itself.

No minimum number of witnesses is created by BSA s.139. The existence of a special SC/ST Act charge does not itself create a special witness-count rule.

9. Production of documents and electronic records

BSA ss.165-168 govern production of documents, use of documents called for and produced on notice, and the Court's power to put questions or order production. Section 165 requires a summoned witness possessing or controlling a document to bring it to Court, subject to the Court deciding objections to production or admissibility. citehttps://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/20063/1/aa202347.pdf

This is materially relevant to Act s.15A rights concerning access to documents/material and to Rules requiring reports and records. Access under the SC/ST Act is not the same legal question as admissibility under the BSA. A document made available to a victim or party still must satisfy the applicable evidentiary rules if relied upon as proof.

10. Evidence-related aspects of Act s.3(2)

10.1 False or fabricated evidence: s.3(2)(i)-(ii)

The statutory wrong is substantive. BSA supplies the proof framework for establishing that evidence was given/fabricated, the accused's intent or knowledge, and the intended or likely consequence concerning conviction. Relevant general provisions include BSA ss.3, 6, 12-13, 54-60, 104-109 and 141-160.

No BSA provision identified in this Phase 1 crosswalk duplicates the special SC/ST Act offence. The offence's punishment and SC/ST-specific victim element remain matters of the SC/ST Act and the applicable current substantive criminal-law transition.

10.2 Disappearance of evidence / false information: s.3(2)(vi)

Section 3(2)(vi) creates a substantive offence where a person, knowing or having reason to believe an Act offence was committed, causes evidence to disappear with the specified screening intent or gives false information concerning the offence with that intent. BSA s.6 on conduct, s.12 on state of mind, s.13 on accidental/intentional conduct, and the general documentary/electronic and burden provisions are materially relevant to proof. citehttps://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/6847/1/prevention_of_atrocities_act.pdf

The BSA does not create a special rule deeming disappearance, destruction, concealment or false information proved. Evidence of such conduct remains subject to the ordinary relevance and proof rules.

10.3 Other s.3(2) aggravated offences

For s.3(2)(iii)-(v), evidence questions generally concern the underlying conduct, intent/knowledge, property/building status, protected person's status and the statutory caste/tribe ground. The BSA supplies the general relevance, oral/documentary/electronic, burden and witness framework. No special BSA provision was identified as duplicating these Act-specific elements.

For s.3(2)(vii), proof of public-servant status and the underlying s.3 offence is governed by the applicable general evidence rules. No distinct special BSA interface was identified.

11. Rules evidence/document interfaces

Rule 5

Rule 5 requires oral information concerning an Act offence to be reduced to writing, read over to the informant, signed and entered in the station record, with a free copy supplied. The Rule is primarily a procedural/recording provision. If the resulting record is tendered in judicial proceedings, BSA rules govern its relevance, documentary/electronic character and proof. BNSS remains the principal procedural interface for FIR/information registration.

Rule 6

Rule 6 requires immediate spot inspection and reports concerning the atrocity, loss of life and property damage, victim/family/dependent identification and protective measures. Such reports may become relevant documents. BSA does not create a special presumption that a Rule 6 report is true or conclusive. Its evidentiary status must therefore be assessed under the BSA and applicable case law.

Rule 7

Rule 7 concerns investigation and charge-sheet timing and does not itself create an evidence rule. Investigation records, statements, documents, photographs, video, electronic records and other material generated during the investigation remain subject to the applicable BSA framework when used as evidence.

Rule 12

Rule 12 requires relief assessment and reporting and contemplates medical and other material in relief administration. These records may have evidentiary relevance, but relief eligibility and proof of the underlying offence are distinct questions. The Rule itself does not create a special presumption as to the truth or admissibility of medical, post-mortem or administrative documents.

Rules 14-15 and implementation records

State review, contingency plans, schemes, reports and implementation records may become documentary evidence where relevant. BSA provisions on public documents, Government notifications, official records and documentary proof apply according to the particular document and its intended use. No general conclusive presumption is created by the Rules.

Rule 18 / Schedule / Annexure material

The Rules Schedule and Annexure material may be relevant to proof of relief entitlement and administrative implementation. Their evidentiary status depends on the nature of the particular notification, schedule, medical or disability material and the purpose for which it is tendered. The existing Schedule item 43 / Annexure-II notification discrepancy remains preserved and is not harmonised in this artifact.

12. Indian Evidence Act transition

The BSA repeal/savings provision creates a proceeding-status distinction:

  • Proceedings pending immediately before 01-07-2024 continue under the Indian Evidence Act as preserved by BSA s.170(2).
  • New proceedings after commencement are governed by the BSA, subject to any applicable special-law rule and other transition provisions.
  • S.O. 2790(E) provides the corresponding-reference mechanism for legacy Indian Evidence Act references in specified instruments in force.

Accordingly, an SC/ST Act proceeding cannot be assigned an evidence regime solely by looking at the date of the SC/ST Act section. The proceeding's status at 01-07-2024 and the operation of BSA s.170(2) must be checked.
