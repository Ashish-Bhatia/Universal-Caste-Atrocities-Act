## 1. Section 3(2)(v): punishment-threshold consequence

The SC/ST Act Schedule and section 3(2)(v) must be read as a special-law overlay on the underlying criminal offence. Section 3(2)(v), as amended in 2015, covers an IPC offence punishable with imprisonment for a term of ten years or more when committed against an SC/ST person or property with the statutory knowledge/ground requirement. The consequence is not a fixed offence list. It is threshold-dependent on the punishment attached to the underlying offence.

The BNS transition therefore creates a material classification question: the corresponding BNS offence must be identified first, then its enacted punishment must be tested against the ten-year threshold. Numerical IPC-to-BNS substitution is not sufficient.

Primary BNS verification establishes examples with direct threshold consequences:

- BNS section 118(2) provides life imprisonment or imprisonment of not less than one year up to ten years, plus fine, for voluntarily causing grievous hurt by dangerous weapons or means. The BNS punishment therefore meets the ten-year threshold in section 3(2)(v), subject to all SC/ST Act elements.
- BNS section 121(2) provides imprisonment of not less than one year up to ten years plus fine for voluntarily causing grievous hurt to deter a public servant. The ten-year ceiling therefore meets the threshold, subject to the SC/ST Act's victim/status and underlying-offence requirements.
- BNS section 124(1) provides imprisonment of not less than ten years up to life plus fine for specified acid/grievous-hurt conduct. This independently meets the ten-year threshold.
- BNS section 119(1) and 120(2), although not Schedule entries, illustrate why threshold analysis must remain offence-specific and must not be inferred from broad offence labels.

The project therefore adopts the following controlled rule: for section 3(2)(v), determine the corresponding BNS offence and its enacted punishment, including the applicable subsection and any alternative/conditional punishment. Do not use the IPC Schedule or old numerical section alone to determine whether the ten-year threshold is met.

Status: primary-text correspondence advanced; a complete offence-by-offence threshold matrix remains a discrete verification task if required for Phase 1 closure.

## 3. Rule 7: BNSS subject-matter correspondence and 60-day consequence

Primary Rules text from the Ministry of Tribal Affairs identifies Rule 7(1) as requiring investigation by a police officer not below DSP rank. Rule 7(2) requires the appointed investigating officer to complete investigation on top priority, submit the report to the SP, requires forwarding to the DGP/Commissioner of Police, and requires the officer-in-charge to file the charge-sheet in the Special/Exclusive Special Court within sixty days, inclusive of investigation and filing. Rule 7(2A) requires written explanation of delay.

BNSS correspondence is subject-matter based:

- Former CrPC section 154 -> BNSS section 173, information in cognizable cases.
- Former CrPC section 156 -> BNSS section 175, police power to investigate cognizable cases.
- Former CrPC section 157 -> BNSS section 176, procedure for investigation.
- Former CrPC section 161 -> BNSS section 180, examination of witnesses by police.
- Former CrPC section 162 -> BNSS section 181, statements to police and use thereof.
- Former CrPC section 164 -> BNSS section 183, recording of confessions and statements.
- Former CrPC section 173 -> BNSS section 193, report of police officer on completion of investigation.
- Former CrPC section 167 -> BNSS section 187, procedure where investigation cannot be completed within twenty-four hours and detention/default-bail architecture.

BNSS sections 4(2) and 5 preserve special-law procedure and special/local laws. Therefore Rule 7's DSP qualification and sixty-day charge-sheet requirement remain special-law requirements. They are not displaced by the general BNSS investigation sequence.

Primary-text consequence of the sixty-day rule: Rule 7 itself establishes a special 60-day investigation/charge-sheet duty and a written explanation requirement for delay. It does not, in its own text, state that failure to file within sixty days automatically creates statutory default bail or changes the custody period under BNSS section 187. BNSS section 187 separately contains the custody/default-bail framework, including the 60-day/90-day distinction tied to the punishment category. Accordingly, the project does not equate Rule 7's 60-day deadline with BNSS section 187's detention entitlement solely by textual comparison.

This resolves the primary-text subject-matter correspondence but not the judicial consequence of breach. The latter requires case-law research and remains outside this Phase 1 primary-text closure. No inference is made from the existence of Rule 7 alone about automatic default bail, invalidity of investigation, or dismissal of prosecution.

## 4. BSA sections 104-109 and 115 versus SC/ST Act section 8

Primary BSA text establishes:

- Section 104: general burden of proof.
- Section 105: burden on the person who would fail if no evidence were given.
- Section 106: burden as to a particular fact lies on the person seeking the Court to believe it, unless another law places it elsewhere.
- Section 107: burden of proving a fact necessary to make other evidence admissible.
- Section 108: burden of proving that the accused's case falls within a general/special exception or proviso.
- Section 109: burden concerning facts especially within the accused's knowledge.
- Section 115: a distinct presumption in disturbed/extensively disturbed areas for specified BNS offences involving armed forces/public-order attacks. It is not a general criminal-law presumption for SC/ST offences.

SC/ST Act section 8 contains three special rebuttable presumptions:

- Section 8(a), after proof of the statutory financial-assistance fact, presumption of abetment.
- Section 8(b), after proof of group commission plus an existing land/other dispute, presumption of common intention or common object.
- Section 8(c), after proof of personal knowledge of the victim or family, presumption of knowledge of caste or tribal identity.

None of BSA sections 104-109 reproduces these SC/ST-specific presumptions. They provide general burden-allocation rules and do not create the special factual triggers in section 8. BSA section 115 is materially unrelated in subject matter and should not be presented as a substitute or equivalent.

The controlled conclusion is therefore: BSA supplies the general evidence and burden framework, while SC/ST Act section 8 remains a special-law presumption regime. The presumptions in section 8 must be applied as special provisions, subject to the Act's text and the BSA's general evidentiary framework. Their judicial operation remains a Phase 2 case-law question.
